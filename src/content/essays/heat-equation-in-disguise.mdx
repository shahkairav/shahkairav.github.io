<p class="dropcap">In 1900, five years before Einstein explained Brownian motion, a doctoral student in Paris named Louis Bachelier modelled the price of French government bonds as a random walk.<Sidenote>Bachelier, *Théorie de la spéculation* (1900). The thesis received the mention *honorable*: respectable, but not the *très honorable* that opened academic doors.</Sidenote> His examiners, Poincaré among them, were more impressed by the mathematics than by the subject. Seventy-three years later Fischer Black and Myron Scholes published the formula that bears their names, and readers noticed something odd: the equation at its heart was one physicists had known for a century and a half.</p>

The resemblance is not a coincidence, and it is not merely formal. It is the same mathematics describing the same phenomenon: the spreading of uncertainty through time. This essay works through the change of variables that makes the identity exact, and then asks what we learn by taking it seriously.

## A walk without memory

Begin with a coin. Each second, a particle steps one unit up or down with equal probability. After $n$ steps its position has mean zero and variance $n$, so its typical distance from the origin grows like $\sqrt{n}$, not $n$.<Sidenote>This is the central limit theorem in its simplest form: a sum of $n$ independent $\pm 1$ steps, divided by $\sqrt{n}$, is approximately standard normal.</Sidenote> That square root is the signature of diffusion: of heat, of ink in water and, Bachelier argued, of prices.

## The pricing equation

Under the usual assumptions,<Sidenote>Constant volatility and interest rate, continuous trading, no transaction costs, and a stock following geometric Brownian motion. None of these is true; how useful the model is remains a separate question.</Sidenote> the value $V(S, t)$ of a European option on a stock with price $S$ at time $t$ satisfies

$$
\frac{\partial V}{\partial t} + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2 \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2} + rS\frac{\partial V}{\partial S} - rV = 0, \tag{1}
$$

## A change of variables

<Theorem kind="definition" title="The heat equation">
A function $u(x, \tau)$ satisfies the *heat equation* if $\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial \tau} = \dfrac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2}$ for all real $x$ and all $\tau > 0$.
</Theorem>

<Theorem kind="theorem" title="Reduction">
Let $x = \ln(S/K) + (r - \tfrac12\sigma^2)(T - t)$ and $\tau = \tfrac12\sigma^2 (T - t)$, and define $u(x, \tau) = e^{r(T - t)}\, V(S, t)$. If $V$ solves (1), then $u$ solves the heat equation.<Sidenote margin>The substitution runs time backwards: $\tau = 0$ at expiry, so the terminal payoff becomes an initial condition.</Sidenote>
</Theorem>

<Proof>
Each substitution removes one term of (1). Multiplying by the discount factor $e^{r(T-t)}$ cancels $-rV$; passing to log-price and shifting by the drift absorbs the first-derivative term; and rescaling time by $\tfrac12\sigma^2$ normalises the diffusion coefficient to one. What remains is $u_\tau = u_{xx}$.
</Proof>

Solving the heat equation with the transformed payoff as initial data, then undoing the substitutions, yields the closed form. It fits in a few lines:

```python
from math import exp, log, sqrt
from scipy.stats import norm

def call(S, K, T, r, sigma):
    # Black–Scholes price of a European call
    d1 = (log(S / K) + (r + sigma**2 / 2) * T) / (sigma * sqrt(T))
    d2 = d1 - sigma * sqrt(T)
    return S * norm.cdf(d1) - K * exp(-r * T) * norm.cdf(d2)
```

## What the identity means

<NewThought>Read the theorem backwards</NewThought> and the value of the option becomes a temperature. The payoff at expiry is an initial distribution of heat along an infinite rod; as we move back in time it diffuses outward, smoothing the kink of the call payoff into the familiar curve.

<Wide>

| In the rod | In the market | Under the substitution |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Temperature $u$ | Option value $V$ | $u = e^{r(T-t)}\,V$ |
| Position along the rod | Log-price, shifted by drift | $x = \ln(S/K) + (r - \tfrac12\sigma^2)(T - t)$ |
| Time since heating began | Time remaining to expiry | $\tau = \tfrac12\sigma^2(T - t)$ |
| Initial temperature | Payoff at expiry | $u(x, 0) = \max(Ke^{x} - K, 0)$ |
| Thermal conductivity | Variance rate | $\tfrac12\sigma^2$ |

</Wide>

<PullQuote>

Volatility is the thermal conductivity of the market.

</PullQuote>

The analogy also marks the model’s limits. Heat obeys conservation laws that prices need not, and a rod’s conductivity does not jump when the rod panics. Much of modern quantitative finance, from local and stochastic volatility to jump processes, can be read as a list of the ways in which a market is not a rod.<Sidenote>Dupire’s 1994 paper “Pricing with a Smile” recovers a local volatility surface from quoted prices: in effect, inferring a position-dependent conductivity from observed temperatures.</Sidenote>
